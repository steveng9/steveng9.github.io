About

I’m a PhD candidate in computer science at the University of Washington Tacoma, where I work with Martine De Cock in the Privacy-Preserving Machine Learning group. I study what a synthetic data release gives away about the people in it. Hospitals, statistical agencies and genomics consortia increasingly share sensitive records by training a generative model on the real data and publishing what the model produces, on the understanding that no real person appears in the output. I build attacks on these releases and use them as measuring instruments. A membership inference attack tells whether a person’s record was used to train the generator, and a reconstruction attack fills in values about a person that were never published.

My membership inference attack, MAMA-MIA, won the 2023 SNAKE challenge and appeared at IEEE SaTML 2025. Earlier attacks trained many copies of the generator to learn what it does; MAMA-MIA instead works out which statistics the generator measured on the hidden training data, which makes it fast enough to audit a release as a matter of course. Our reconstruction attacks won NIST’s 2025 Collaborative Research Cycle and grew into an SoK at PoPETs 2027. scMAMA-MIA (NeurIPS 2026) carries the approach to synthetic single-cell genomic data, where earlier attacks had done little better than guessing, and shows that these releases leak the most when they come from only a few donors, as many single-cell studies do.

Across these projects, the measurements keep showing that a release’s protection is spread unevenly over the people in it. A differential privacy guarantee gives the whole dataset one number, ε, and the results suggest that the same ε covers a record in a dense region far better than a record out on its own. Next, I want to predict each record’s exposure from the shape of the data before any attack is run, and to spend the privacy budget where that shape needs it. The research page has the details.

Before the PhD, I wrote software for Boeing’s flight simulator. I’ve played percussion with Symphony Tacoma since 2017.

    Research

    Is the release safe, and for whom?

    I study privacy-preserving data releases the way an auditor would, by building the adversary.

    Every synthetic data release carries a promise, and the promise contains two questions. The first is whether the release is safe: can anyone learn something about a real person from it that they could not have learned without that person’s data? The second, asked far less often, is whether it is equally safe for everyone in it, and when it fails, who is exposed first. I design attacks on these releases and use them as measuring instruments for both questions. A membership attack on synthetic cancer data, for example, could reveal that a person had cancer.

    Thread 2

    Who is exposed first?

    Averages hide their tails.

    A privacy audit usually reports one number, an average over every record. In our SoK, synthetic data from the diffusion model TabDDPM looks close to safe on average, with a mean row-level reconstruction advantage of 1.4%. Broken down by race, the same release exposes American Indian and Alaska Native individuals (1.1% of the data) at 8.4%, against 1.2% for the White majority. Few synthetic records share a rare combination of public attributes, so each one that does maps back to a single real person. People whose public profiles are the most unusual, as judged by an outlier detector that sees nothing else, are reconstructed 3.4 times as well as everyone else.

    The single-cell work finds the same pattern along a different axis. Leakage grows as the number of donors shrinks, and on the AIDA immune atlas with 20 donors (about 43,000 cells) our attack reaches an AUC of 1.00. Differential privacy behaves differently: in the SoK it kept every subgroup within 1.5 times of its mean, which is the evenness its guarantee promises. Across three kinds of data and more than twenty generators, the measurements keep finding that a release’s protection is spread unevenly over the people in it, and that where a person sits in the data decides how much of it they get.

    Thread 3 · next

    One ε, a shaped object

    Where I want to take this as faculty.

    A differential privacy guarantee is one number, ε, handed to an entire dataset. Data has shape, and the results above suggest that the same ε gives a record in a dense region far more cover than a record out on its own. I want to work on three things. The first is predicting each record’s exposure from the geometry of the data before any attack is run, from quantities such as local density and how well connected a record is to its neighbors. The second is differentially private mechanisms, with proofs, whose noise follows that shape. In single-cell data, for example, much of a cell’s state is shared across donors (its cell type, common biology), and only the donor-specific deviation identifies anyone. The third is privacy for data that is itself a graph of people, such as a pedigree or a referral network, where even deciding what counts as one person’s contribution is a choice about shape.

    These are directions with first experiments planned, not results yet. The first one is cheap: compare each donor’s connectivity in a nearest-neighbor graph against how often scMAMA-MIA catches them.

    Fairness you can edit in

    NeurIPS 2026 workshop, FAccT version in progress.

    Whoever generates a synthetic dataset chooses the generating process, and a process can be edited. DECAF showed that three fairness definitions become three sets of edge cuts on one GAN’s causal graph. We ported the cuts to nine generators from three unrelated families (marginals-based, GAN, and diffusion, each with differentially private variants), over 2,520 matched-pair runs on the Adult and COMPAS datasets. The mechanism transfers to all of them. Our causal diffusion backbone produced the fairest release of any family we tested, the cut barely moves fidelity, it costs a downstream classifier about 0.07 to 0.15 AUC on average, and adding privacy guarantees doesn’t make the data less fair.

    To work out what a cut does on graphs small enough to check by hand, I built a causal graph lab that runs in the browser. You draw the graph, mark which nodes are protected, admissible, or hidden, and generate a table from it. The lab then shows which paths from the protected attribute to the outcome each fairness definition cuts, how much of the fairness gap each path carries, and what the cut costs a model trained on the table. It also loads the column sets of the 11 benchmark datasets from our experiments.

    Also

    Computing on data no one can see

    Secure computation and federated learning, from 2022 to the iDASH 2026 win.

    Before synthetic data, I worked on cryptographic routes to the same goal. Our federated system for detecting fraudulent bank transfers, built on privacy-preserving membership queries across institutions, took second place in the US–UK PETs Prize Challenge and became a PoPETs 2024 paper. A decentralized data marketplace that keeps both buyers’ inputs and sellers’ outputs private appeared at the SIGMOD Data Economy Workshop in 2023. In 2026 our team won first place in Track III of the iDASH privacy and security competition, which asked for fast two-party secure inference of a drug–target interaction model on GPUs. A pharmaceutical company’s candidate drugs are its intellectual property, so the drug is split into secret shares held by two servers, and neither server learns it. We designed SlimDTA, a model that performs fewer operations on the secret drug (in particular fewer of the nonlinear ones, which need the servers to communicate), and a GPU framework that evaluates the linear layers on additive shares and the nonlinear layers with function secret sharing. Ours was the fastest of the four finalist solutions on all three of the organizers’ tests, at 8.1 to 9.2 ms per prediction, and its predictions match the plaintext model to within 0.02%.

      Teaching and mentoring

      Start where the student is

      I presented my master’s thesis about a dozen times: to my committee, at a conference, on a poster, to undergraduates, to a room of university administrators, in thirty minutes and in three. I went in assuming these audiences sat along one line, from non-technical to technical, and that each new version meant sliding along it. The line turned out not to exist. The administrators had no reason to know what a marginal distribution was, but they knew far better than I did what happens to an institution when records it promised to protect turn up somewhere they shouldn’t. The undergraduates could have computed a marginal in a few lines of Python, and had never wondered whether a dataset could be anonymous in the first place. Each audience was missing a different piece, so each version needed a different way in (a picture for one, a worked example for another, an analogy for a third).

      The first thing I try to do as a teacher is still to find out what the person in front of me already understands, which is rarely what I would have guessed, and start there.

      In a course

      I finished a bachelor’s and a master’s degree without AI, and now use it every day in research to get other people’s code running and to manage experiments. A student in an introductory course doesn’t have that ordering available, and I think it shapes what a course should ask of them.

      So on the first day I would ask students what they expect from the course and which tools they already use, and how, and then be just as direct about my own expectations. I would grade the process along with the finished program, since the finished program is now the cheapest part to produce. And I want to try an inverse oral exam: I explain the assignment aloud, carefully and more than once, and the student’s job is to turn what they heard into a written specification and then a working system. Whether or not they use AI, that translation is the work.

      I also try to be an easy person to ask. Part of what makes a chatbot attractive is that it is easier to ask than a person, and office hours only compete with that if asking me is easy too.

          Hierarchical trades

          Agents that never move sit on a map where each region is rich in one resource and poor in the others, so they have to trade. Once a trade earns a surplus, another agent can build a trade on top of it. The question is whether hierarchies of institutions emerge from local trading, and what they do once they have.
